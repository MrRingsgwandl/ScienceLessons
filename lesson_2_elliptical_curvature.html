Core Orbit Geometry

Defining Oval Pathways

Vocabulary Mastery

Click each card to expand our scientific meanings and oval geometry terms:

Ellipse
A closed geometric curved shape resembling an oval. Unlike a circle which has one center point, an ellipse is generated around two internal anchor points called foci.
Foci (Focus points)
Two specialized points inside an ellipse. In any orbital path, the massive central star sits precisely at one focus point, while the second focus remains completely empty.

Have students understand that a circle is actually just a perfect ellipse where the two foci merge into a single central point.

Core Orbit Velocity

Defining Dynamic Space Speeds

Vocabulary Mastery

Click each card to expand terms describing speed variation in space:

Perihelion
The exact point along an orbital path where a planet is closest to the Sun. At perihelion, the pull of gravity is strongest, and the planet moves at its maximum speed.
Aphelion
The exact point along an orbital path where a planet is furthest from the Sun. At aphelion, gravity is at its weakest, and the planet moves at its slowest speed.
Eccentricity
A scientific scale from 0 to 1 measuring how stretched out an ellipse is. 0.0 is a perfect circle, while values closer to 1.0 represent heavily flattened ovals.

Introduce the Greek roots: 'Peri' (near), 'Ap' (away), and 'Helios' (Sun) to help students easily remember these terms.

Kepler's First Law

The Discovery of Ellipses

EXPLICIT TEACHING

Breaking the Circle

For over a thousand years, scientists believed planets moved in perfect circles. However, in 1609, astronomer Johannes Kepler proved that orbits are actually ellipses.

The First Law: All planets move in ellipses, with the Sun sitting at one of the two focus points, rather than in the exact center.

Mapping the Orbit:

Think of drawing an ellipse with a piece of string and two pushpins. The pins represent the two focus points. The farther apart they are, the more stretched (eccentric) the orbit becomes.

Does Earth have a high or low eccentricity?

Discuss how Mars has a noticeably eccentric orbit, which is what led Kepler to reject perfect circular systems.

Kepler's Second Law

Equal Areas in Equal Times

EXPLICIT TEACHING
Kepler's Equal Area sweep model. Short & wide wedge vs. long & narrow wedge.

The Sweeping Area Law

Kepler discovered that an imaginary line connecting a planet to the Sun sweeps out **equal areas of space in equal intervals of time**.

  • Close to Sun: The planet moves fast, drawing out a short but wide wedge.
  • Far from Sun: The planet moves slow, drawing out a long but narrow wedge.

Explain that because the area of Area 1 equals Area 2, the planet must cover the outer perihelion curve much faster than the aphelion curve.

The Gravity Well Analogy

Why Orbits Speed Up

Analogy Mapping

Scientists often represent gravitational pull using a flexible **rubber sheet** to model space fabric warping:

  1. Massive Sun: Forms a deep central dip in the warped sheet fabric.
  2. Orbital Planet: Rolls around the slopes of this warped dip.
  3. Speed Boost: As the rolling object dips deeper, it converts potential gravity energy into raw kinetic velocity.

Have students visualize spinning coins down a spiral wishing well. Relate the extreme boost in speed at the bottom directly to perihelion.

Evaluating Scientific Models

Critiquing Exaggerated Kepler Diagrams

CRITICAL EVALUATION

Model Strengths

  • Clearly illustrates aphelion/perihelion points.
  • Makes the geometric concept of foci easy to visualize on flat slides.
  • Shows changes in planetary velocity in a clear, observable way.

Model Limitations

  • Orbits of actual planets are *almost* perfect circles (very low eccentricity).
  • Exaggerated diagrams can make students think Earth's seasons are caused by moving closer to or further from the Sun (which is false!).

Crucial check: Remind students that Earth's distance from the Sun changes by only 3%, meaning our seasons are caused by axial tilt, not orbital eccentricity.
